Racing thoughts at 2 a.m. A tight chest before every email from your ex. A sense that something terrible is about to happen, even on an ordinary Tuesday. If this sounds familiar, you’re not alone. Anxiety is one of the most common experiences after a divorce.

Why divorce can trigger anxiety

Divorce changes almost everything at once: where you live, how you spend your days, your finances, your friendships, sometimes your sense of who you are. Your brain is wired to treat that much uncertainty as a threat, so it stays on high alert, scanning for danger and replaying worst-case scenarios.

In other words, anxiety after divorce is often your nervous system trying to protect you. Understanding that can make it feel a little less frightening.

What it can look like

  • Constant worry, especially about money, the children or the future
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep
  • Irritability, restlessness or feeling “on edge”
  • Physical symptoms like a racing heart, tight chest or upset stomach
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions

What helps

Calm your body first

When anxiety spikes, thinking your way out rarely works. Start with your body. Breathe in for four counts and out for six, five to ten times. Put your feet flat on the floor and notice five things you can see. Take a brisk ten-minute walk. These small resets signal safety to your nervous system.

Give worries a time and a place

Set aside fifteen minutes a day as “worry time.” Write down everything on your mind, then sort it: what can I act on this week, and what is out of my control? When a worry shows up at another time, jot it down and tell yourself you’ll look at it later.

Protect your sleep and routine

Regular wake times, daylight in the morning, less caffeine after noon and less alcohol in the evening all help steady anxiety more than most people expect.

Limit the triggers you can

If messages from your former spouse send your heart racing, check them at set times rather than all day, and wait before replying. Keep communication brief and focused on logistics.

Anxiety is a signal, not a sentence. With support, it can quiet down.

When to get help

Consider talking with a therapist if anxiety is interfering with sleep, work or parenting, if you’re avoiding things you need to do, or if it has lasted more than a few weeks. Anxiety responds well to therapy, and you don’t have to wait until it’s severe. If you ever feel unsafe, call or text 988.
