Loneliness after divorce can show up in surprising places: setting one plate at dinner, a quiet Sunday afternoon, having no one to tell about your day. Sometimes it’s strongest not when you’re alone, but when you’re surrounded by couples.

If you feel lonely, please know it isn’t a personal failing. It’s a natural response to losing a daily companion and, often, a shared social world. And it can change.

Why loneliness is common after divorce

Over a long marriage, many friendships become couple friendships. Holidays, weekends and routines were built around two people. After a divorce, some friends drift away, some take sides, and some simply don’t know what to say. Add a quieter home, perhaps an empty nest, and the gap can feel enormous.

1. Name what you’re missing

Loneliness isn’t one thing. You might miss conversation, physical affection, someone to share plans with, or simply a sense of belonging. Naming it helps you look for the right kind of connection.

2. Reach out first, even when it’s uncomfortable

Many friends want to help but don’t know how. Make it easy for them: “Would you like to get coffee Saturday?” Aim for one small reach-out each week. Connection is built in small, repeated moments.

3. Join something that meets regularly

Friendships grow from seeing the same people again and again. Look for something with a regular rhythm: a walking or fitness group, a book club, a class, a faith community, a volunteer shift, or a divorce support group.

Being alone and being lonely aren’t the same. You can learn to enjoy one and ease the other.

4. Make time alone feel good

Turn solo time into something you choose rather than endure. Cook a real meal for yourself. Take yourself to a museum or the beach. Build small rituals, like a morning walk or Sunday tea, that make home feel like yours.

5. Be gentle with screens

Scrolling can make loneliness feel worse, especially when other people’s lives look picture-perfect. Notice how you feel after time online, and use your phone to connect directly, through a call or a message, rather than just watching.

6. Take care of your body

Sleep, movement and daylight all support mood. When we feel physically depleted, reaching out feels much harder.

When to get support

If loneliness comes with persistent sadness, hopelessness or withdrawing from people you care about, it’s worth talking to someone. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 right away.
