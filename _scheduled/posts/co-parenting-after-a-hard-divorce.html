The marriage is over, but the parenting isn’t. If your divorce was painful, the idea of working with your former spouse for years to come can feel exhausting, or even impossible.

Here’s the encouraging truth: you don’t have to like each other, or even agree on much, to co-parent well. What children need most is for the conflict between their parents to stay away from them. That is something you can work toward, starting today, even if your ex doesn’t.

1. Keep the conflict away from your children

Research on children and divorce points to the same thing again and again: it isn’t the divorce itself that harms children most, it’s ongoing conflict between their parents. That includes arguments in front of them, tense handoffs, and being asked to carry messages or take sides.

  • Never use your children as messengers. Speak to your co-parent directly, or in writing.
  • Don’t criticize their other parent in front of them, even when it’s deserved.
  • Keep pickups and drop-offs short, calm and polite.

This applies to teenagers and young adults too. Older children often understand more, which can make it even harder for them to feel caught in the middle.

2. Treat communication like a business relationship

It can help to think of your co-parent as a colleague you don’t have to like, but do have to work with. Keep messages brief, informative, friendly and firm. Stick to logistics: schedules, school, health, activities.

“Pickup is at 5 on Friday. Please let me know by Wednesday if anything changes. Thanks.”

Before you send anything, ask yourself: would I be comfortable if a judge, or my child, read this one day? If not, wait an hour and rewrite it.

3. Use tools that lower the temperature

Shared online calendars and co-parenting apps keep schedules, expenses and messages in one place, with a written record. Many parents find that moving conversations out of texts and phone calls reduces arguments, because there is less room for tone and misunderstanding.

4. Keep routines as steady as you can

Children feel safer when life is predictable. You won’t be able to make both homes identical, and you don’t need to. Aim for agreement on the big things, like bedtimes on school nights, homework expectations and screen time, and let the small differences go.

5. When co-parenting isn’t possible, try parallel parenting

If every conversation turns into a fight, a different approach may work better. In parallel parenting, each parent runs their own household with very little contact between them. Communication is kept to the essentials, usually in writing, and decisions follow a clear, agreed plan. It isn’t a failure. For high-conflict situations, it can be the healthiest option for everyone.

If you have concerns about safety, abuse or your children’s wellbeing, speak with a family law attorney, and contact local authorities or the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) if you are in danger.

6. Take care of yourself, too

Co-parenting after a hard divorce can stir up old hurt every week. Notice what triggers you: a certain tone, a late text, a change of plans. Have a plan for those moments, like the 4–6 breath (in for four counts, out for six), a walk, or calling a friend before you reply. The calmer you are, the calmer your children will be.

When to get support

Co-parenting counseling can help you and your former spouse agree on a plan, communicate with less conflict, and focus on what your children need. Even if your co-parent won’t take part, individual therapy can help you respond rather than react, and protect your own peace.

You can’t control your co-parent. You can control what your children see from you.
