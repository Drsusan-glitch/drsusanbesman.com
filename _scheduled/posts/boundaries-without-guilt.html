After a divorce, it can feel like everyone has an opinion and a claim on your time: your former spouse, your adult children, your parents, your friends. If you’ve spent years keeping the peace, saying no can feel almost impossible, and saying it without guilt even more so.

Boundaries aren’t walls, and they aren’t punishments. They’re clear, kind statements about what you will and won’t do. They protect your energy so you can show up as your best self for the people who matter.

Why boundaries feel so hard right now

Many women were raised to be accommodating, and long marriages can reinforce that pattern. After a divorce, you may also feel you have to prove you’re handling everything well. So you say yes when you mean no, then feel resentful or exhausted. That’s a sign a boundary is needed, not a sign you’re a bad person.

1. Notice where you feel drained

Resentment, dread and exhaustion are useful signals. Write down the situations that leave you feeling that way: a certain kind of phone call, a recurring request, a topic someone keeps raising.

2. Decide what you need

For each situation, ask: what would make this feel manageable? Maybe it’s talking to your ex only by email. Maybe it’s not discussing the divorce at family dinners. Maybe it’s a limit on how often you host.

3. Say it simply

You don’t owe a long explanation. Short, calm sentences, repeated as often as needed, work best.

“I’m not going to discuss that. Let’s talk about something else.”
  • With a former spouse: “I’m happy to talk about the children’s schedule. I won’t discuss our relationship.”
  • With adult children: “I love you, and I’m not going to share the details of the divorce.”
  • With family: “I know you mean well. I’ve made my decision, and I’d appreciate your support.”
  • With your time: “I can’t this weekend, but I’d love to see you next month.”

4. Expect pushback, and hold steady

People who are used to your old patterns may test the new ones. That doesn’t mean your boundary is wrong. Calmly repeat it. Over time, most people adjust.

5. Let guilt be a feeling, not a verdict

Guilt often shows up simply because you’re doing something new. Notice it, name it, and remind yourself why the boundary matters. Then do something kind for yourself with the time and energy you’ve protected.

When to get support

If boundaries feel impossible, or someone repeatedly ignores them, a therapist can help you practice the conversations and stay steady. In therapy we often rehearse the exact words together.
