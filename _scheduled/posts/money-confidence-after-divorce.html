For many women, money is one of the scariest parts of divorce. Maybe your spouse handled the finances. Maybe you’re worried about retirement, or about keeping the house. Or maybe you’re perfectly capable but still lying awake doing math at 3 a.m.

I’m not a financial advisor, and this article isn’t financial advice. But as a therapist, I see how much the emotional side of money affects women after divorce. Building confidence is as much about feelings as it is about numbers.

Why money feels so emotional after divorce

Money is tied to safety, independence and identity. After a divorce, it can carry fear (“Will I be okay?”), grief (“This isn’t the future we planned”), and sometimes shame (“I should know more about this”). Those feelings are understandable, and they can make it hard to look at the numbers at all.

1. Start by looking, gently

Avoidance is the most common response to money anxiety, and it usually makes the anxiety worse. Choose a calm time, make a cup of tea, and simply gather the facts: income, regular expenses, accounts, debts and insurance. You don’t have to solve anything yet. Just look.

2. Build a team of experts

You don’t have to figure this out alone. Depending on your situation, that team might include a family law attorney, a financial professional who specializes in divorce, such as a Certified Divorce Financial Analyst, and a tax professional. Ask questions until you understand. Good professionals welcome that.

3. Learn one thing at a time

Pick one topic a month: your budget, your retirement accounts, your credit, your insurance. Small, steady learning builds real confidence, and it’s never too late to start.

Financial confidence isn’t knowing everything. It’s knowing where you stand and what to ask.

4. Slow down big decisions

In the early months after a divorce, emotions can push us toward quick decisions, like selling the house or making a large purchase to feel better. Where you can, give big decisions time and good advice.

5. Notice your money stories

Many of us carry beliefs about money from childhood or marriage: “I’m bad with money,” “Someone else should handle this,” or “There’s never enough.” Notice those thoughts and ask whether they’re still true. Often, they were never true at all.

6. Celebrate progress

Opening the statements, making your first budget, asking a question you were afraid to ask: each one is a real step toward independence.
